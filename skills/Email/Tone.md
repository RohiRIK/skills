# Tone: register and culture

Pick one register per email and keep the greeting, the body, and the sign-off inside it. A mixed register ("Dear Sir," and then "Cheers!") reads as careless or machine-made.

## Choosing the register

Start from the relationship, then adjust. Money, bad news, and legal topics move one notch more formal. On a reply, mirror the other person and never go less formal than they were.

| Relationship | Default register |
|---|---|
| Government office, court, municipality, insurer, bank, legal dispute | Formal |
| New external contact, client, vendor, senior stranger, recruiter | Professional |
| Known colleague, ongoing client | Friendly |
| Close teammate, quick internal ping | Casual |

## Register ladder

| Register | English greeting | English sign-off | Hebrew greeting | Hebrew sign-off |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Formal | `Dear Ms. Levi,` · `Dear Claims Team,` | `Kind regards,` · `Yours sincerely,` | `לכבוד` block, then `שלום רב,` | `בכבוד רב,` |
| Professional | `Hi Dana,` · `Hello Dana,` | `Best regards,` · `Best,` | `שלום דנה,` | `בברכה,` · `תודה,` |
| Friendly | `Hi Dana,` | `Thanks,` · `Best,` | `היי דנה,` | `תודה!` · `יום טוב,` |
| Casual | `Hey Dana,` · `Dana,` | `Thanks!` · name only | `היי,` · `דנה,` | `תודה!` · name only |

English notes:
- "Dear Dana," is normal in UK and European business and slightly stiff in US tech. "Hi" is the safe professional default.
- "Kind regards" is British-neutral, "Best regards" neutral, "Best" American, "Cheers" British or Australian casual.
- "Yours sincerely" when you know the name; "Yours faithfully" (UK) only after "Dear Sir or Madam", which is itself a last resort.

Hebrew notes:
- Israeli business Hebrew is first names with almost everyone, including senior people. "שלום רב" signals distance and formality; next to a first name ("שלום רב דנה") it reads stiff, so write "שלום דנה,".
- "בברכה" is the safe default sign-off. "בכבוד רב" belongs to formal letters and authorities. "תודה," works as the sign-off on any email that asks for something.
- "[שם] יקר," is a calque of "Dear X" and sounds like marketing mail. Natural exception: "לקוחות יקרים" in customer announcements.
- Day-aware greetings are natural, never required: "בוקר טוב" in the morning, "שבוע טוב" from Saturday night through Sunday, "שבת שלום" or "סוף שבוע נעים" on Thursday and Friday, "חג שמח" in a holiday week.

## Tone dials, inside a register

- **Directness**: point first, always. Softeners only where the reader's culture expects them (below).
- **Warmth**: at most one human line, and specific: "thanks for turning the deck around overnight" beats "thanks for everything".
- **Urgency**: the date and the consequence, never adjectives. "I need it by Thursday 12:00 to file on time" instead of "URGENT" or "ASAP".
- **Emoji and "!"**: none in Professional and Formal, except one "Thanks!". Friendly and Casual follow the thread.
- **Humor**: it rarely survives email or translation. Leave it out of hard messages entirely.

## Culture bridge: Israel and English-speaking business

Israeli business writing is direct, informal, and short. US and UK readers can take that as curt or even rude. The reverse also fails: US-style padding reads to Israelis as fake or evasive.

| Writing to | Do | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| US or UK, from Israel | Turn asks into questions ("Could you…?"), add one courtesy line and a specific thanks, frame a no as "we can't do X, but we can do Y" | Bare imperatives ("Send me the file"), "you must", ALL-CAPS urgency, skipping the greeting |
| Israel, from US or UK | Put the ask in line 1, use first names, cut the warm-up, keep it short | "I hope this email finds you well", stacked pleasantries, heavy hedging ("I was just wondering if maybe…") |
| Anyone else | Professional register, mirror their reply | Idioms and slang that don't translate |
